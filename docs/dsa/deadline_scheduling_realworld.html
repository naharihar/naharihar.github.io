01 — Hotel Front Desk

The question behind "Is this room available?"

Grand Hyatt · Front Desk · 3:47 PM

You walk up. The receptionist turns to her screen. "Do you have a room from the 14th to the 18th?"

A moment passes. "Yes, we have a king available on the 12th floor."

What did the computer just answer?

At its core, the system is answering one question: does your requested stay conflict with any existing reservation for that room type? Think of each reservation as a pair [checkIn, checkOut], and your request as another pair: [14, 18]. The question becomes: does your interval overlap with any existing one?

Two intervals conflict if and only if they overlap. The overlap condition is:

Overlap condition: intervals [a, b] and [c, d] overlap when a < d AND c < b.

The clearest way to derive it: first identify when they do not overlap — either your stay ends before theirs starts (b ≤ c), or theirs ends before yours starts (d ≤ a). Negate both conditions and you get the overlap formula.
Pause and think

A hotel has 80,000 reservations across all rooms. Your check-in is the 14th. For a given room, how would you quickly find whether any existing booking overlaps [14, 18] — without scanning all bookings?

For the coding problem (LC 729 — My Calendar I): keep bookings for a room in a sorted data structure. To check [14, 18], find the first booking whose start is less than 18 and check whether its end is greater than 14.

This is exactly what LC 729 asks you to implement. The interval overlap condition is the core of the solution.

How real hotel systems approach this

The conceptual model above — a sorted list of bookings per room, checked with binary search — is exactly what LC 729 tests. But production hotel systems at any meaningful scale are unlikely to work this way. Availability needs to be fast for millions of concurrent searches.

One way production systems approach this

A common pattern is to pre-aggregate availability rather than scan booking records directly. One simplified version: maintain a table with one row per room type per date, tracking an available count. Checking availability for [14, 18] might then look like this:

-- Simplified illustration — real queries vary significantly
SELECT MIN(available_count)
FROM   inventory
WHERE  room_type_id = 42
  AND  date BETWEEN '2024-01-14' AND '2024-01-17';
-- If MIN > 0, the room is available for the whole stay

This touches only as many rows as there are days in the requested stay. The overlap logic moves into how counts are decremented when a booking is made, rather than being checked at query time.

Concurrency is the hard part. The real challenge isn't checking availability — it's handling two guests simultaneously checking the same room. Production systems typically address this with database-level locking or optimistic concurrency controls.

02 — The Exam Center

How many cubicles does a testing center need?

Prometric Testing Center · 9:12 AM

You arrive for your GRE. There are 24 individual testing cubicles. Most are occupied. The proctor checks you in: "You're in cubicle 7. Your exam starts at 9:15."

Three things are worth asking: How did 24 become the number? When someone finishes early, how does the system decide who gets their cubicle next? And why does your exam slot have a hard expiry window?

Question 1: How many cubicles?

The relevant algorithmic question is: given a set of exam bookings, what is the maximum number running simultaneously at any point? That peak is the minimum number of cubicles needed.

Sort by start time. Use a min-heap of end times. Process each exam in start-time order. When a new exam begins, check if the smallest end time in the heap is ≤ the new exam's start. If so, that cubicle is free to reuse. Otherwise, add a new cubicle.

This is LC 253 — Meeting Rooms II. Cubicles are meeting rooms. Exams are meetings. The algorithm is identical.

The algorithm vs. the real decision

Physical capacity decisions involve demand forecasts, building layout, licensing rules, and budget. The min-heap algorithm is still the correct conceptual tool for answering "what peak concurrency does the data imply?"

Question 2: Who gets the next free cubicle?

When cubicle 7 frees up at 11:00 AM and three candidates are waiting, which one should be assigned first? This is where deadline scheduling enters the exam center.

Each candidate has a testing window. If Candidate A's window closes at 1 PM and Candidate B's closes at 5 PM, giving the cubicle to A first is the right call.

Earliest Deadline First (EDF): always assign the available resource to the candidate whose window expires soonest. If any assignment schedule can serve all candidates within their windows, EDF will find one.

EDF maximizes the number of candidates served, not fairness to any individual.

This maps directly to LC 630 — Course Schedule III: sort by deadline, use a max-heap to swap out the longest course if time exceeds a deadline.

The fleet-sizing pattern in other domains

The same "how many resources do I need?" question appears everywhere: ICU beds, rental fleets, courtrooms, cloud servers. All of these reduce to: given N intervals of resource usage, what is the maximum number overlapping at any moment?

03 — University Course Registration

Picking courses is a scheduling optimization problem

University Portal · Registration Week

You open the course catalog. You want to take Algorithms, Computer Networks, Linear Algebra, and Film Studies. But Algorithms is Mon/Wed 10–11:30, Networks is Mon/Wed 10–12. Which combination do you register for?

You're doing in your head what a scheduling algorithm does formally.

This is interval scheduling directly. Each course is an interval [startTime, endTime] on the weekly schedule. You want to maximize the number of courses you can take without overlap.

The greedy insight

Among all available courses that don't conflict with what you've already committed to, always pick the one that ends earliest. This leaves the maximum remaining time available for future courses.

Weighted courses: If courses have different credit values and you want to maximize total credits, the problem becomes weighted interval scheduling and requires dynamic programming combined with binary search.

Sort by end time: [9,11], [10,12], [11,13], [12,14], [13,15], [14,16].

Take [9,11], then [11,13], then [13,15]. Maximum = 3 courses.

How registration systems typically approach this

University registration systems usually do reactive conflict checking when a student tries to add a course. The optimization problem is typically left to the student or to planner tools.

The greedy algorithm is what an optimal human strategy looks like, formalized.

04 — Ride-Share Dispatch

Driver assignment as a resource scheduling problem

Ride-share app · 9:03 AM · You request a ride

You open the app. A driver is assigned in seconds. Why that driver and not another one three blocks away?

Distance is one factor — but so is availability.

Thinking about it algorithmically: each driver is a resource, and each trip is an interval of time during which that resource is occupied. The dispatch question has real structural similarity to resource scheduling.

The EDF intuition in dispatch: Among all resources currently occupied, the one whose end time is soonest becomes available first. Tracking that is the scheduling insight. Whether any given system implements it this way is a separate question.
What production systems actually do

Real dispatch systems combine spatial indexing, ML models for trip duration prediction, supply-demand balancing, and pricing signals. The min-heap model is a useful conceptual frame, not a description of a production implementation.

The LeetCode problem that captures the resource-assignment structure most cleanly is LC 2402 — Meeting Rooms III. The structure is a good analogy for dispatch, even if the real-world system is more complex.

05 — Pattern Summary

Four scenarios, one underlying question

Strip away the hotel lobby, the exam cubicle, the registration portal, and the driver app and you find the same skeleton every time: items claim segments of a shared resource, and you need to decide which claims to honor, how many resources to provision, or who gets the next free slot.
